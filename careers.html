Careers

There is no single cybersecurity job

Security teams need investigators, builders, reviewers, researchers, writers, organizers, and people who can translate between them. A better starting question than “How do I become a hacker?” is “What kind of problems do I want to work on?”

Labor-market figures checked August 2026. Salary data describes groups of workers, not an offer or a guaranteed starting salary.

Why people study security

The subject rewards curiosity about how things work, but it also has a practical purpose: helping people use technology without accepting avoidable harm. The work changes often, crosses many disciplines, and can lead into engineering, investigation, public policy, law, education, or research.

Read job statistics carefully

“Information security analyst” is only one occupation in a much larger field. National medians combine experience levels and locations. They do not mean that a new graduate will begin at the median.

For local context, the BLS reported a $139,930 annual mean wage for information security analysts in the New York–Newark–Jersey City metropolitan area in May 2022. This is older, broad tri-state data and a mean rather than a median, so it is useful as context, not as a promise about a New York City starting salary.

What the roles actually involve

Security operations and incident response

Monitor alerts, investigate suspicious activity, contain incidents, collect evidence, coordinate recovery, and improve detections afterward. Useful foundations include networks, operating systems, logs, scripting, and calm written communication.

Security engineering

Build and operate controls such as identity systems, endpoint protection, network segmentation, logging pipelines, secrets management, and cloud guardrails. This path usually benefits from ordinary systems or software engineering experience.

Application and product security

Work with developers to threat-model designs, review code, test applications, manage vulnerabilities, and make secure behavior easier to implement. Strong candidates understand software development as well as attack techniques.

Cloud security and identity

Design permissions, secure cloud workloads, review configurations, automate policy, and investigate identity misuse. Learn one cloud platform, but also learn the underlying concepts: networks, roles, keys, logs, containers, and infrastructure as code.

Penetration testing and red teaming

Test systems with permission, document realistic attack paths, and explain how to fix them. The report and the safety of the engagement matter as much as the exploit. Many advertised “ethical hacker” roles expect years of broad technical experience.

Governance, risk, compliance, and privacy

Translate laws, contracts, standards, and business needs into workable controls. The job may include risk assessments, audits, vendor reviews, policy, privacy analysis, and executive communication. Technical literacy still matters even when the daily work is not code.

Threat intelligence, research, and forensics

Study adversaries or vulnerabilities, analyze malware and systems, develop methods, and communicate evidence with appropriate uncertainty. These roles often require deeper specialization.

Ways into the field

Few people begin with “cybersecurity” in every part of their title. Common starting points include IT support, network operations, software development, systems administration, auditing, data analysis, military or government service, internships, and research.

  1. Build a foundation in computers, networks, one scripting language, and clear technical writing.
  2. Try two or three areas through small projects before committing to a specialty.
  3. Read real job descriptions and make a list of repeated skills. Ignore impossible wish lists; look for patterns.
  4. Create evidence of work: a repository, lab notes, a detection rule, a secure application, or a short incident report.
  5. Ask people about their daily work, not only their title. Titles vary sharply between organizations.
  6. Apply before you feel perfectly qualified, then use interviews and rejections as information about what to study next.
College, certifications, and projects answer different questions

A degree can provide breadth and access to internships. A certification can show that you studied a published body of knowledge. A project can show how you approach a real task. None proves everything, and none replaces judgment, reliability, or communication.

Projects that help someone evaluate you

  • Blue team: collect Windows or Linux logs, create three detections, generate test events, and write a triage guide.
  • Networks: diagram a small lab, segment it, document firewall decisions, and capture a normal connection from DNS through TLS.
  • Software: build a modest web app with authentication, tests, dependency scanning, structured logging, and a short threat model.
  • Cloud: deploy a disposable service using infrastructure as code, least-privilege roles, centralized logs, budgets, and a teardown procedure.
  • Risk: choose a fictional school service, identify assets and threats, recommend controls, and explain the tradeoffs to a nontechnical reader.
  • Research: reproduce a public vulnerability in an isolated lab and explain the root cause without publishing harmful targeting details.

A strong project has a question, a constrained scope, evidence, limitations, and instructions another person can follow. Screenshots alone do not explain what you understood.

What employers keep testing

Fundamentals

Can you explain the system beneath the security tool?

Judgment

Can you distinguish a serious risk from an interesting but unlikely one?

Communication

Can someone act on your report without guessing what you mean?

Reliability

Do you document, test, protect data, and follow the rules of an engagement?
